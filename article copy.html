From Africa to America's Energy Future: Mandlaenkosi Mthetwa Is Powering a Cleaner, Smarter Grid

Dr. Tendai Chikowore

Energy Policy Analyst

June 14, 2024 8 min read 18,450 views

From a small town in South Africa to the laboratories and city halls of the United States, Mandlaenkosi Mthetwa's journey is a compelling story of how global talent is quietly shaping the future of clean energy. Now a graduate of the Rochester Institute of Technology (RIT) in New York, Mandla is leading research that tackles a challenge many cities around the world face but rarely rethink: how to keep essential public buildings powered during outages without relying on noisy, polluting diesel generators.

Brighton town hall in Rochester, New York wants to replace its backup generator with a battery storage system. There are different types of batteries that can be used for storage purposes. However, some types of batteries such as lead-acid batteries have chemicals that negatively impact the environment. Over the last years, lithium-ion batteries have seen a decrease in price which has made it easy for these types of batteries to be used for storage purposes.

Innovative Research with Real-World Impact

His Brighton Town Hall backup power study explores whether modern battery systems—especially second-life electric vehicle batteries—can provide a cleaner, smarter alternative for municipal resilience in the U.S., using real-world data and economic comparisons rather than abstract theory.

"What makes this research stand out is not just the technology, but the mindset behind it. Instead of assuming that 'green' solutions are automatically cheaper or better, Mandla carefully compares new batteries, repurposed EV batteries, and traditional generators to understand what actually works today."

Researchers testing second-life EV batteries for energy storage applications.

His work shows that while diesel generators still dominate on cost in the short term, second-life batteries offer a powerful glimpse into a circular energy future, where electric vehicle batteries are reused instead of discarded, reducing waste and emissions while improving energy security. This kind of evidence-based thinking is exactly what cities, utilities, and policymakers need as they plan for climate resilience and aging infrastructure.

A Scholar's Journey Across Continents

Behind this research is a young African scholar whose achievements reflect both excellence and perseverance. Mandla graduated from RIT with a Master's degree in Sustainable Systems and an outstanding GPA of 3.87, after earning multiple merit-based scholarships that supported his education journey across three continents—from Zimbabwe and Turkey to the United States.

He has been recognized repeatedly for academic excellence, including full international scholarships, top-of-faculty honors in electrical engineering, and competitive Erasmus funding that took him to Delft University of Technology in the Netherlands. These milestones highlight not only personal brilliance, but the global value African students bring to science and engineering when given opportunity.

Modern energy research laboratories are becoming hubs for international collaboration.

From Academia to Industry Impact

Today, Mandla's work extends beyond academia into U.S. industry and federally supported research, where he contributes to advanced battery technologies with applications ranging from clean transportation to grid resilience. His story is a reminder to Africa's tech ecosystem that innovation is not limited by geography—ideas born in African classrooms can influence energy systems in American cities and beyond.

As African Tech continues to spotlight global changemakers, Mandlaenkosi Mthetwa's research stands as a powerful example of how African talent is helping to design a cleaner, more resilient energy future for the world. The implications extend beyond Brighton Town Hall, offering a blueprint for municipalities worldwide seeking to transition from fossil-fuel-dependent backup systems to smart, sustainable alternatives.

The Circular Economy Opportunity

The research highlights a critical juncture in energy storage economics:

  1. Cost Trajectory: Lithium-ion battery prices have fallen 89% since 2010, making stationary storage increasingly viable.
  2. Second-Life Potential: EV batteries typically retain 70-80% capacity when retired from vehicles, suitable for less demanding stationary applications.
  3. Environmental Benefits: Repurposing batteries reduces mining demand and keeps hazardous materials out of landfills.
  4. Economic Model: Creates value streams throughout battery lifecycle, benefiting both EV manufacturers and storage operators.

As cities worldwide face increasing climate-related power disruptions, the research led by Mandlaenkosi Mthetwa provides timely, data-driven insights for building resilient, sustainable energy infrastructure that serves communities while protecting the planet.

Can Rural Africa Capitalize on Used EV Batteries for Mini-Grids?

Rutendo Moyo

Clean Energy Consultant

June 13, 2024 7 min read 22,180 views

Across rural Africa, millions of households still live without reliable electricity not because renewable energy is unavailable, but because energy storage remains expensive and difficult to deploy at scale. As solar mini-grids spread across sub-Saharan Africa, batteries have become the single most decisive factor in whether these systems succeed or fail. Mandlaenkosi Mthetwa's research in the USA tackles a timely question with global implications: can retired electric-vehicle (EV) batteries, which still retain much of their capacity, be repurposed to power rural African mini-grids sustainably and affordably?

The idea has attracted growing attention from policymakers and practitioners. According to Dr. Tendai Chikowore, an energy policy analyst and former advisor on rural electrification programs in Zimbabwe, "Second-life EV batteries represent a potential game-changer for Africa's mini-grid sector, but only if decisions are grounded in rigorous economic and technical analysis. What this research does well is move the discussion beyond optimism to evidence."

Economic Realities and Tipping Points

Mandla's study shows that while used EV batteries are cheaper upfront, their faster degradation and shorter lifespan can make them more expensive over time than new batteries under current market conditions. However, the research also identifies a clear tipping point: if second-life battery prices fall sufficiently, they could become the most cost-effective storage option for rural communities.

"Africa does not need blanket solutions, we need context-specific choices. Studies like this help governments and developers understand when second-life batteries make sense and when they do not, which is essential for protecting both public funds and rural communities."

Solar mini-grid installation in rural Zimbabwe - storage remains the key cost component.

This nuance is critical for African energy planning. Ms. Rutendo Moyo, a Zimbabwe-based clean-energy policy consultant who has worked with mini-grid developers in Mashonaland and Matabeleland, notes that "Africa does not need blanket solutions, we need context-specific choices. Studies like this help governments and developers understand when second-life batteries make sense and when they do not, which is essential for protecting both public funds and rural communities." Her point underscores a central message of the research: second-life batteries are not a silver bullet, but a conditional opportunity.

Beyond Economics: Policy and Practical Challenges

Beyond economics, the study raises important policy questions about safety, logistics, and environmental responsibility:

  1. Quality Control: Establishing certification standards for second-life battery health and performance.
  2. Transportation Logistics: Cost-effective and safe transport from global markets to rural African sites.
  3. Technical Capacity: Training local technicians to maintain and monitor repurposed battery systems.
  4. End-of-Life Management: Developing recycling infrastructure to handle batteries after their second life.
  5. Regulatory Framework: Creating policies that encourage sustainable reuse without becoming dumping grounds for low-quality batteries.

Quality testing of second-life batteries is essential for safe deployment in rural areas.

Turning Global Transition into Local Advantage

With the right safeguards and price structures, Africa could turn the global EV transition into a local advantage using technology retired in wealthy markets to accelerate energy access at home. The research identifies several scenarios where second-life batteries make economic sense:

  • Short-term backup: Applications requiring less frequent cycling and lower depth of discharge.
  • Hybrid systems: Combining second-life batteries with other storage or generation sources.
  • Community-scale projects: Where upfront cost constraints outweigh long-term lifecycle costs.
  • Demonstration projects: Pilot installations to build local expertise and test business models.

Mandlaenkosi Mthetwa's work offers a grounded roadmap for how rural Africa might capitalize on this opportunity—carefully, strategically, and sustainably. As the global EV fleet grows exponentially in coming years, Africa has the chance to develop a circular energy economy that addresses both energy poverty and environmental sustainability.

The Path Forward

The research suggests several policy interventions that could make second-life batteries more viable for Africa:

Regional Testing Centers: Establishing facilities to certify battery health and performance before deployment.

Standardized Procurement: Creating bulk purchasing mechanisms to reduce costs through economies of scale.

Training Programs: Developing vocational training for local technicians in battery management and maintenance.

Partnership Models: Fostering collaborations between African mini-grid developers, global EV manufacturers, and research institutions.

As Africa continues its energy access journey, evidence-based approaches like this research will be crucial for making smart investments that deliver lasting benefits to rural communities while contributing to global sustainability goals.
